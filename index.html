Who we are

We spent years being told the matrix wasn't good enough

Matrix Engineering makes creasing matrix and ejection rubber on the Isle of Man, and supplies folding carton converters and die-makers worldwide.

The complaints came to us

Before 2009 we supplied creasing matrix to this industry, which meant we were the ones who took the call when it lifted, when it crushed, when it left residue on the plate. Converters and die-makers told us plainly, year after year, that it was not good enough.

They were right. And we knew exactly why, because we knew what was going into it. Hearing the same faults from the same trade for long enough leaves you with a very clear specification for what a matrix ought to be.

So in 2009 we built the line ourselves, on the Isle of Man, and specified the polymer and the adhesive ourselves rather than accepting what a supplier offered. Everything that leaves here is extruded by us. Nothing is bought in and relabelled, which means every tolerance is ours to hold and ours to answer for.

On the bench

Fitting matrix in six steps

  1. Clean the plate

    Degrease and dry the cutting plate or counter jacket. Anything left on it will show up in the crease.

  2. Cut to length

    Use a proper matrix cutter at 45° or 90°. A clean end sits down flat.

  3. Locate on the rule

    Push the locator bridge down over the creasing rule so the channel sits square.

  4. Peel the backing

    Lift the fingerlift tape to expose the adhesive, keeping the strip in place.

  5. Take an impression

    One cycle at normal pressure transfers the matrix onto the plate.

  6. Remove the bridges

    Lift the locators out of the channel and you're ready to run.

Troubleshooting

What goes wrong, and why

We spent years taking the calls when creasing went wrong, before we made anything ourselves. This is what we learned. Some of it points at the matrix — including at ours — and most of it does not. We would rather tell you either way than sell you something that will not fix it.

Crease cracks when it is folded

The crease looks clean coming off the press and splits on the folder-gluer.

What is usually behind it

The board has not delaminated properly. Creasing works by breaking the bond between the inner plies so the fibres can move; if that has not happened, the top liner is asked to stretch over a fold it cannot take. Usually the channel is too wide, or the crease rule is sitting too low to drive the board far enough into it.

What to try

  • Drop to the next channel width down — a narrower channel breaks the plies up more evenly.
  • Check the rule height before touching pressure. A low rule is the more common culprit.
  • Check the grain. Creases running across the grain crack far more readily than with it.
  • Board below about 8% moisture will crack whatever you do. Worth a check before blaming the tooling.

The crease bursts on the press

Fibres split along the crease as it is cut, not later.

What is usually behind it

The opposite problem: too much force for the fibre to carry. Either the channel is too narrow for the caliper, the rule is too high, or the press is simply running heavier than the job needs. It shows up worst where creases sit close together, because the board is being pulled from both sides at once.

What to try

  • Go one channel width up, or take the rule height down — but not both at once, or you lose the crease altogether.
  • On tightly spaced creases, reducing rule height by 0.1–0.2mm is usually enough.
  • Come back down on impression. Burst creases are more often pressure than tooling.

The matrix lifts off the plate

It moves during the run, or comes away with the sheet.

What is usually behind it

Nearly always the surface, not the adhesive. Anything left on the plate from the last job — residue, oil off a cloth, dust — and the tape is sticking to that rather than to steel. A plate that has never been degreased is the usual story.

What to try

  • Degrease and dry the plate properly before laying anything down. It is the single biggest cause.
  • Press the full length down, not just the ends; the middle is what lifts first.
  • If it keeps happening on a clean plate, tell us. That is our problem, not yours, and we want to know.

Residue left behind at changeover

The matrix comes up but the adhesive stays on the plate.

What is usually behind it

The adhesive has been formulated to hold rather than to release, which is a choice the manufacturer made and you are living with. It is the fault we hear about most, and it is the one we designed Phoenix+ around: high shear under pressure, clean lift at changeover.

What to try

  • If you are scraping plates after every job, the matrix is wrong. Ask us for samples and run them side by side.
  • Lift from one end at a steady angle rather than picking at the middle.

The matrix crushes or loses its profile

Creases go shallow part-way through the run and the shoulders spread.

What is usually behind it

The base is too narrow for the load it is carrying. On heavy solid board and micro-flute a standard footprint will move under the platen, and once the shoulders spread the channel is no longer the width you specified.

What to try

  • Move to a wider base — that is what Phoenix XL exists for.
  • Check the plate underneath. A pitted or worn plate concentrates load and will crush anything.
  • If it is crushing on a sound plate at sensible pressure, the material is not good enough. Ours should not.

The crease is off-centre

One shoulder is deeper than the other, and the carton folds skew.

What is usually behind it

The rule and the matrix channel are not aligned. It happens most often after the chase or the cutting plate has been pulled out and put back, and it is worth checking every single time rather than once a week.

What to try

  • Re-check alignment after any chase or plate movement.
  • An off-centre profile is a deliberate product, not a symptom — if the job needs one, use one.
  • Even where the spine does not split, skewed folds change the finished carton size and cause trouble down the line at cartoning.

Splitting at the corners of a crease

The crease is sound along its length and fails where it meets another.

What is usually behind it

The matrix has been cut square and butted up to its neighbour, so the two ends fight each other at the junction and there is nowhere for the fibre to go.

What to try

  • Chamfer the ends at 45° when you trim to length. It takes seconds and it is the fix.
  • Cut with a proper matrix cutter. A clean end sits flat; a crushed one does not.

Angel hair and dusting

Fine fibres around the cut edge, all over the sheet and into the machine.

What is usually behind it

A dull rule is the first suspect: a sharp blade lets the board fracture, a worn one pins and tears it. After that, a worn cutting plate, too much impression, and ejection rubber that has gone hard and is letting the waste burst apart rather than pushing it cleanly.

What to try

  • Change the rule before anything else, and check the plate for pitting.
  • Press a fingernail into your ejection rubber. If it does not spring back, it is done — and that is the rubber's fault, not the board's.
  • Recycled board has shorter fibres and will always dust more. It is not necessarily a fault.

Ejection rubber goes hard

It worked when it went on and now it does not push the waste out.

What is usually behind it

Most rubber degrades — heat, UV and time harden it, the rebound goes, and make-ready creeps up as the operator compensates with pressure. Exceed holds its profile past 1.6 million impressions rather than going off in the rack.

What to try

  • If you are replacing rubber on a schedule rather than on wear, you are buying the wrong rubber.
  • Hard rubber and angel hair usually arrive together. Fix the rubber and watch the dusting.
